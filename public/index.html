Capability Provenance in Language Models

A Case Study in Social Reasoning

Glenn Matlin🐝🔥🛡️ Chandreyi Chakraborty🐝 Saehee Eom🐝 Mika Okamoto🐝 Rayan Castilla🐝 Louis Jaburi🕊️ Alvin Deng🕊️ Taywon Min🤖🔥 Lucia Quirke🕊️ Stella Biderman🕊️ Mark Riedl🐝

Paper arXiv Code

Where does a language model's social reasoning come from? We trace skills back to the training data that supports them. Affective, informal, conversational text supports social reasoning; math and STEM reasoning draw on different text. And when a model is made to forget the flagged data, the matching skill weakens more than when it forgets random data.

Abstract

Training data as a capability map.

Language models learn everything they can do from their training data, but which data taught which skill is usually invisible. We use training-data attribution to make it visible: starting from a capability, we trace back through the model to the kinds of training text that support it.

We ask one focused question: does social reasoning — figuring out what people want, feel, and intend — come from different text than STEM reasoning? We label every document in a fully open training corpus by topic and format, score how strongly each region of the corpus supports each skill, and then check the answer causally by making the model forget flagged regions and re-testing it.

The answer is yes. Social reasoning draws on conversational, affect-rich, people-centered writing; STEM reasoning draws on technical, expository material. We measured this first in OLMo3, and we are re-running the same pipeline across a family of open-data models to test how far the pattern generalizes.

Method

From documents to interpretable corpus regions.

The central move is aggregation: every benchmark query is traced back to many individual documents, then summarized into comparable topic-format regions.

The core tool

What is an influence function?

The model answered a question correctly. Which parts of its training data taught it that? An influence function estimates how the model's answer would change if one training document had been left out — without retraining the model.

During training, every document nudges the model's internal settings a little. A benchmark question also defines a direction: what would make the model better at this? Documents whose nudge points the same way have positive influence — they supported the skill. Documents whose nudge points the opposite way score negative.

One document's score is noisy. Average over thousands of documents that share a topic and format, and the noise fades into a readable map of where a skill comes from. These are estimates, not proofs — which is why the pipeline ends with an unlearning check.

Corpus taxonomy

24 topics crossed with 24 formats.

The corpus is de-duplicated and every document is labeled by what it is about (topic) and what kind of writing it is (format), using an off-the-shelf taxonomy (WebOrganizer). Sampling evenly across the occupied bins turns attribution from a document retrieval problem into a structured corpus comparison problem.

Contrastive design

Domain and capability type are separated.

The 2 by 2 design asks whether social reasoning is merely social-domain knowledge, or whether reasoning tasks use different corpus regions than knowledge tasks in the same domain.

If reasoning and knowledge in the same domain draw on the same documents, the two pairs should separate identically. They do not, which is the question finding 03 takes up.

Data

Four benchmarks, one fully open corpus.

All four probes are OLMES-standard multiple-choice benchmarks, evaluated 5-shot: SocialIQA (social reasoning), MMLU Social Sciences (social knowledge), ARC-Challenge (science reasoning), and MMLU STEM (STEM knowledge). For the OLMo3 deep-dive, query gradients come from the instruct model on the bare questions, while document gradients are computed on the base model, keeping the analysis tied to pretraining data.

The corpus side is what makes the study possible: every document the model trained on is open and available for analysis. That is billions of unique documents after de-duplication, from which we draw a stratified sample of several million spread across all topic-format bins — the raw corpus is heavily skewed toward a few large ones.

Findings

Social reasoning has a distinct provenance signature.

01

Different skills, different data.

We compared how each benchmark draws on the labeled corpus. Social reasoning stands apart: the kinds of text it relies on look unlike the kinds the STEM and social-knowledge tasks rely on, and those three resemble each other. First measured in OLMo3; the same comparison is being re-run on every model we add.

How we measured it: for each pair of benchmarks, we correlated their influence profiles across the whole topic-format taxonomy.

02

The text behind social reasoning is conversational, affective, and people-centered.

Reading the high-influence text itself, it comes in two flavors: interactional writing — dialogue, questions and answers, feelings and intentions, lots of "you" and "I" — and expository writing such as guides and manuals about how people deal with each other. Math and STEM reasoning draw instead on structured, technical text. This lexical pattern, not any single corpus slice, is the claim we expect to hold across models.

03

The social-versus-STEM split is sharper for reasoning tasks than for knowledge tasks.

Knowledge tasks ask the model to retrieve facts about a domain; reasoning tasks ask it to work over one, so the two stress different training text. The social-minus-STEM contrast is wider for the reasoning pair than for the knowledge pair, while leaning the same direction in both.

At the topic level, reasoning leans social on topics like literature and education, and leans STEM on topics like science & technology and software development.

04

The causal check: forgetting the flagged data weakens the matching skill.

Correlation alone does not prove the flagged text matters, so we test it causally. We make the model unlearn the high-influence regions and compare against unlearning randomly chosen regions from the same topics. Influence-targeted forgetting damages social reasoning more than the random controls; for the other tasks the effect is weak or absent.

Unlearning validates load-bearing corpus regions in aggregate; it does not identify individually causal documents or a full mechanism.

Models

Does the pattern hold beyond OLMo3?

The pattern was measured first in OLMo3. The same pipeline is being re-run across a family of open-data models — different corpora, different token budgets, same question.

Limits

What this study does and does not claim.

  • Attribution is an analytic lens, not an exact proof of causal necessity for individual documents; unlearning validates aggregate patterns without eliminating approximation error.
  • Analysis runs on a stratified working set sampled from the full corpus; results should not be read as characterizing every document the model saw.
  • Unlearning shows a corpus region materially supports the aligned benchmark; it does not explain the mechanism by which those documents shape model behavior.
  • The full deep-dive is measured on one open-data ecosystem (OLMo3 / Dolma3). Extensions to OLMo3 Instruct, Marin, DCLM, and Comma — and 32B-scale runs — are in progress; until they land, generalization across model families remains an open question.

The contrastive design probes the structure of capability provenance. It does not evaluate, or make claims about, the social reasoning abilities of any population or demographic group.
